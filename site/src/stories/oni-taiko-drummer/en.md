## legend

When summer thunder rolls over Japan, grandparents still tease children with an old warning: cover your belly button, or the thunder god will take it. His name is Raijin. In old paintings and temple statues he looks like an oni, one of the horned ogres of Japanese folklore, wild-haired and fierce, with a ring of taiko drums floating around him. When he beats them with his sticks, thunder booms across the sky. Beside him often flies his companion Fūjin, the god of wind, with a bag of storm winds over his shoulders.

Oni themselves are fearsome creatures of legend, with horns and tusks, tiger-skin loincloths and heavy iron clubs. Every year at Setsubun, on the eve of spring, families throw roasted beans out of the door and shout, "Oni out! Good luck in!" Yet an oni face can also be turned against evil. In many villages, dancers put on fierce oni masks to frighten bad spirits away.

The drum, too, has a long story. For centuries taiko have sounded at shrines and temples, calling people together and carrying their prayers. Warlords used them on the battlefield to signal their troops. In farming villages a festival without drums was hardly a festival at all. The great odaiko, carved from a single tree trunk and headed with stretched cowhide, can be taller than a man, and its boom rolls through the chest like thunder.

On Sado Island, in the Sea of Japan, the two ideas meet in a tradition called ondeko, the demon drum. At spring festivals, dancers in oni masks leap and stamp to the beat of a taiko, going from house to house to drive off evil and pray for a good harvest.

Taiko drummers today still tie a hachimaki around their heads, sink into a deep, low stance and throw their whole body into every stroke. Between beats they shout, a sharp cry called kakegoe, to keep the rhythm and fire up the crowd.

So picture a summer night at a shrine festival. Paper lanterns sway on their rope, the red torii glows under a crescent moon, and the crowd waits in the dark. On the stage stands an oni with a red face and a white mane, two sticks in his fists. He raises them high, and when they fall, the thunder comes down to earth.

## beats

- idle: The oni waits on the festival stage beside the great drum, two sticks loose in his fists, lanterns swaying overhead.
- raise: He lifts both arms high with the sticks pointed back, like Raijin gathering a storm.
- hit: The first blow crashes onto the drumhead, a ring of sound ripples out, and the paper lanterns swing.
- lift: He swings the sticks back up overhead, ready to bring down the next thunderclap.
- hit: The second blow lands harder, a wider ring rolls out, and the lanterns sway again.
- lift: Up go the sticks once more as the drum still hums from the last stroke.
- hit: The third blow booms across the shrine grounds like thunder rolling off the hills.
- wind up: He leans back into his biggest wind-up, arms and sticks stretched far behind his horns.
- double: Two blows fall in quick succession, and the thunder doubles across the shrine grounds.
- shout: He flings his arms up and roars a kakegoe, the drummer's cry, into the night.
- recover: He lowers his sticks and settles back into his stance as the lanterns calm.
