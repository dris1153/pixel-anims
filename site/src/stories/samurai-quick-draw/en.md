## legend

A samurai wore his sword thrust through his sash, sheathed, with the edge turned up. And that raised a question every swordsman had to answer: what if the enemy attacks before the blade is even drawn? From that question grew iaijutsu, the art of drawing the sword and cutting in one single movement.

Tradition names Hayashizaki Jinsuke, a swordsman of the late sixteenth century, as its founder. The story goes that he set out to avenge his murdered father, and that after long days of prayer and training at a shrine, the secret of the swift draw came to him. Many schools that teach the art today trace their line back to him.

In iaido, as the art is practised now, every form follows the same path. First comes the draw and cut, nukitsuke, the blade leaving the scabbard and striking in the same breath. Then comes the finishing cut. Then chiburi, a sharp flick that shakes the blood from the blade. Last of all comes nōtō, sliding the sword back into its scabbard, slowly and surely. Through it all the swordsman keeps zanshin, the remaining mind, his attention still on the enemy even after the fight is done. The old masters said the finest victory is the one won with the sword still in its scabbard.

And the cherry blossom? Every spring, cherry trees across Japan burst into bloom, and within a week or two the petals are falling. For centuries poets have seen in them how short and beautiful a life can be. Warriors took the flower as their own: like a petal that falls at the height of its beauty, a samurai was meant to be ready to fall in his prime, without clinging and without regret. An old saying puts it simply: "Among flowers, the cherry blossom; among men, the warrior."

So picture a hill in spring, a pagoda in the far hills and a cherry tree in full bloom. A swordsman stands beneath it, perfectly still, as petals drift down around him. Then, faster than the eye can follow, the blade is out and back again, and the petals fall in halves.

## beats

- idle: The samurai stands perfectly still on the blossom hill, his hand resting on the hilt as petals drift down.
- stance: He sinks into a deep crouch, feet wide, steadying the scabbard at his hip, ready to draw.
- slash: In a single flash the blade leaves the scabbard and cuts across the screen, slicing the falling petals.
- sheathe: He flicks the blade clean and slides it home into its black lacquered scabbard.
- settle: He rises from the stance and stands still again, keeping zanshin as the petals keep falling.
