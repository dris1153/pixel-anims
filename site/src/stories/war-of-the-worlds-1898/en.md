## legend

No one would have believed, in the last years of the nineteenth century, that our world was being watched from across space by minds far greater than ours, vast and cool and unsympathetic, and that slowly and surely they were drawing their plans against us. So begins the story, told by a quiet writer who lived at Woking, south-west of London.

For several nights astronomers saw strange flashes of fire on the red planet Mars. Then a falling star streaked over the south of England and came down on Horsell Common, in the sandpits near the writer's home. By morning a crowd stood around a huge metal cylinder half buried in the sand, still too hot to touch, and watched in wonder as its round top slowly turned, unscrewing itself from within.

Out crawled the Martians: grey, rounded bodies as big as bears, glistening like wet leather, with great dark eyes, a lipless mouth and a tangle of tentacles. A small party walked toward the pit waving a white flag. A flash of invisible heat leapt out, and they burst into flame.

Soon the Martians built their fighting machines: tripods of glittering metal, taller than houses, striding over the young pine trees on three jointed legs, with long steel tentacles swinging below a hooded body. Each carried the Heat-Ray, which set woods ablaze, melted lead like water and turned houses to ruins in a single sweep. Guns and soldiers could do little. The machines fired canisters of choking black smoke that rolled over the villages along the Thames, and the people of London fled in their millions.

Behind the invaders spread the red weed, a plant from Mars that crept over the ruins, choked the rivers and stained the land the colour of blood. The narrator, trapped for days in the wreck of a house, watched it grow and felt that mankind was no longer master of the earth.

When at last he crept into an empty London, he heard a strange howl, "Ulla, ulla, ulla," and then it stopped. On Primrose Hill the fighting machines stood silent, and black birds were tearing at the dead Martians below. After all the weapons of men had failed, they had been slain by the humblest living things on Earth: the germs of our air and water, against which their bodies had no defence. Soon the red weed sickened and died too.

The people came home, and the writer found his wife alive. But the world had lost its easy confidence, for now everyone knew the sky could hold enemies.

## beats

- common: At dusk villagers stroll on the common near Woking while Mars glows red and low on the horizon.
- cylinder: A green flash streaks down, the cylinder smashes into the sandpit in a spray of sand, and its lid slowly unscrews.
- tripod: A Martian fighting machine rises on three long jointed legs, its hooded cowl high and steel tentacles dangling.
- heatray: The Heat-Ray sweeps from its box: the pines burst into flame, a house glows and collapses, and people flee.
- smoke: Black smoke rolls from the tripod over the village as a second machine strides along the Thames.
- redweed: The red weed from Mars creeps over the ruins and chokes the river banks.
- fall: The tripods stop, sway and topple, killed by earthly germs, and crows settle on their cowls.
- green: Green returns over the red weed, church bells ring, people walk home, and Mars rises again at dusk.
