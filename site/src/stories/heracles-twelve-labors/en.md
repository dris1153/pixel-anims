## legend

Heracles was the son of Zeus and the mortal Alcmene, and the goddess Hera hated him from his birth. She sent a fit of madness upon him, and in its grip he harmed his own family. When his mind cleared, he went in grief to Delphi, where the Pythia, the priestess of Apollo, told him to serve King Eurystheus of Tiryns and carry out ten labors. When they were done, she promised, he would become immortal.

First came the Nemean lion, whose hide no weapon could pierce, so Heracles choked it with his bare hands and wore its skin ever after. The Hydra of Lerna had nine heads, and for each one he cut off, two grew back, until his nephew Iolaus seared the stumps with a torch and Heracles buried the last, immortal head under a rock. He chased the golden-antlered hind of Artemis for a whole year, and drove the Erymanthian boar into deep snow to carry it home alive. He cleaned the stables of King Augeas in one day by turning two rivers, the Alpheus and the Peneus, through them. At Lake Stymphalus he shook bronze rattles from Athena, and when the birds burst out of the woods, he shot them down.

Then the labors took him to the ends of the earth. He captured the bull of Crete, tamed the man-eating mares of King Diomedes, and won the war belt of Hippolyta, queen of the Amazons. Far in the west he took the red cattle of Geryon, a giant with three bodies, and on the way he raised the Pillars of Heracles at the edge of the world.

But Eurystheus refused to count the Hydra, because Iolaus had helped, or the stables, because Heracles had asked Augeas for payment. So ten became twelve. For the golden apples of the Hesperides, Heracles held up the sky while the Titan Atlas fetched them. Atlas wanted to stay free, so Heracles asked him to take the sky back for a moment while he eased a pad onto his shoulders. Atlas did, and Heracles walked off with the apples. Finally he went down to the underworld and, with no weapon, dragged the three-headed hound Cerberus up into the daylight.

The Greeks painted these deeds again and again on their vases, black figures on orange clay.

Years later, in agony from a poisoned robe, Heracles built a pyre on Mount Oeta and lay down on it. As the flames rose, a cloud and a crash of thunder carried him up to Olympus, where he became a god and married Hera's daughter Hebe. And the stars kept his shape: the constellation Hercules still kneels in the night sky.
