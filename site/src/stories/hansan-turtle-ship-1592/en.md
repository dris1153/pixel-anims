## legend

In the spring of 1592, the armies of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the ruler who had united Japan, crossed the sea and landed in Korea. It was the start of the Imjin War. On land they marched north so fast that within weeks the king had fled and the capital had fallen. But at sea the war went another way, because of one admiral: Yi Sun-sin.

Yi commanded the Jeolla Left Navy, based at Yeosu on the south coast. His warships were panokseon, broad and sturdy, with two decks: the rowers pulled their oars safe below while archers and gunners fought from the deck above. And he had the turtle ship, the geobukseon, an old design he had rebuilt just before the war. Its back was a curved roof bristling with iron spikes, so no one could leap aboard. Cannons looked out from ports along its sides, oars drove it from below, and the dragon head at its bow, it is said, poured out smoke to hide it as it came.

That summer a Japanese fleet under Wakisaka Yasuharu, some seventy ships by Korean count, lay in the Gyeonnaeryang strait, a narrow channel of rocks and shoals between Geoje Island and the mainland. Yi came with his fellow admirals Yi Eok-gi and Won Gyun and fifty-odd ships. He would not fight inside the strait. It was too tight for his big ships to turn, and a beaten enemy could simply run ashore. He wanted the battle in the open water off Hansan Island.

So on 14 August he sent in a handful of panokseon as bait. They fired, turned and rowed away as if afraid. The Japanese ships poured out of the strait after them, into the wide sea, where the rest of Yi's fleet was waiting.

Then the drums boomed and the signal flags went up. The fleeing ships wheeled round, and the whole Korean fleet spread into a great curve, like a crane opening its wings. This was the hagikjin, the crane-wing formation, and its two wings swept forward and closed around the Japanese fleet. The cannons fired one after another, the turtle ships, two or three of them by most accounts, drove into the line, and fire arrows rained onto the decks. Ship after ship caught fire and went down.

By the Korean records, forty-seven Japanese ships were sunk and twelve captured. Wakisaka escaped with the ships that could break away. Hansan is counted among the great naval victories of history. It kept Korea's western sea lanes safe, so the invading armies in the north could not be supplied by sea, and it made the name of Yi Sun-sin a legend.

## beats

- dawn: At summer dawn Admiral Yi Sun-sin stands in armor on his flagship's pavilion beside the war drum, his fleet hidden behind Hansan Island.
- strait: The Japanese fleet streams out of the narrow Gyeonnaeryang strait at the left, ship after ship under square sails.
- lure: Five panokseon row out as bait, fire one volley, then turn and flee, and the enemy gives chase.
- open: The chase spills out of the strait into the wide open water before Hansan Island.
- drum: The war drum booms, signal flags run up, and Yi's hidden fleet surges out from behind the island.
- crane: The Korean ships wheel into the hagikjin, the crane-wing formation, its two wings sweeping round to close on the enemy.
- turtle: A turtle ship bursts forward, smoke pouring from its dragon head and its spiked roof gleaming, and rams into the enemy line.
- cannon: All along the arc the cannons fire in a rolling broadside, and cannonballs splash among the ships.
- fire: Fire arrows streak across the water, sails burn, masts fall and smoke darkens the sky.
- rout: The surviving ships break away and flee toward the strait, leaving burning wreckage drifting on the water.
- victory: At sunset Yi's fleet gathers in line, flags streaming, and the sailors raise their oars in a cheer over the red sea.
- return: Night falls, the fires die, and the ships slip back behind the island under the moon, ready to begin again at dawn.
