## legend

In Heliopolis, the City of the Sun, the ancient Egyptians honoured a sacred bird called the bennu. It looked like a grey heron with two long feathers trailing from its head, and it belonged to the sun. They said that at the dawn of time, when the first land rose from the dark waters, the bennu flew over the waters and landed on the sacred benben stone, and its cry broke the silence and set time going. Like the sun, it was always renewed, and its name was linked to an Egyptian word meaning to rise and shine.

The Greeks heard of a wondrous bird of the sun from Heliopolis, and they called it the phoenix. The historian Herodotus, who travelled in Egypt in the fifth century BCE, wrote down what the priests of Heliopolis told him. The phoenix came only once every five hundred years. It was shaped like an eagle, with feathers of red and gold. When its father died, it made an egg of myrrh, laid the father inside and carried him all the way from Arabia to the temple of the sun. Herodotus admitted he had only seen the bird in paintings, and did not quite believe the tale.

Later poets added more. The Roman poet Ovid wrote that the phoenix lives on drops of incense, and at the end of its long life builds a nest of cinnamon, myrrh and spices at the top of a palm tree and dies there among the sweet scents. From its body a young phoenix is born, which carries the nest to the temple of the sun.

Then came the fire. In later tellings, such as the Physiologus, an early book of animal lore from Alexandria, the phoenix flies to the altar of the sun at Heliopolis and burns itself up in the flames, and within three days it rises new from its own ashes. That is the phoenix most people know today.

Because it dies and is born again, the phoenix became a symbol of hope that nothing truly ends. Early Christians saw the resurrection in it, cities rebuilt after great fires put it on their flags and seals, and storytellers still call on it whenever something rises again from ruin.

## beats

- perch: At dusk on a volcanic ridge, the phoenix rests on an old stone altar, like the altar of the sun.
- spread: It opens its red and gold wings wide as the ember glow of the ridge flickers around it.
- blaze: The bird bursts into flame from crest to tail, burning away its long old life.
- ash: The flames die down and the phoenix crumbles into a heap of glowing ash on the altar.
- rebirth: An ember egg pulses in the ashes, like the egg of the old tales, and a young phoenix rises from it.
- fold: The newborn phoenix settles on the altar and folds its wings, ready for the long centuries ahead.
