## legend

Carnival came to Brazil with the Portuguese, as the last wild feast before the forty quiet days of Lent. In colonial Rio it was a rough game called the entrudo, when people ran through the streets pelting each other with water, flour and little wax balls full of scented water. Later the wealthy held masked balls in the European style, and grand carnival societies paraded through the city on decorated floats.

Meanwhile, in the old streets near Praça Onze, something new was being born. Families from Bahia, many of them descended from enslaved Africans, had settled there, and in the homes of women like Tia Ciata musicians gathered to play and dance through the night. Out of African rhythms and the songs of the city came samba. In 1928 sambistas from the Estácio neighbourhood founded Deixa Falar, often called the first samba school, and soon neighbourhoods and hillside communities across Rio had schools of their own, each with its own colours and its own pride. By the early 1930s they were competing in a parade.

Today the great schools parade through the Sambadrome, a long avenue between grandstands, designed by Oscar Niemeyer and opened in 1984. Each school chooses a theme and a samba written for it, and several thousand members pass in little more than an hour, judged on everything from the drums to the costumes. The front commission opens the story with a small, dazzling dance. Floats as tall as houses roll by, crowned with sculptures and dancers. The mestre-sala and the porta-bandeira, the school's most honoured couple, carry its flag: she spins with it held high while he dances around her, guarding it. The baianas, older women in wide round skirts, whirl in memory of the Bahian aunts who sheltered samba in its early days. And the bateria, hundreds of drummers led by their master, keeps the whole school moving on one great heartbeat of surdo drums.

The parade goes on all night, school after school, and often the sun is rising before the last one leaves the avenue. Then the street sweepers in orange come out to clear the confetti, sometimes dancing samba with their brooms, and the crowd cheers them too. On Ash Wednesday the scores are read out, and one school is crowned champion of the year.

## beats

- bateria: The drum corps marches in, white and green, and the whole frame pulses to the beat of the big surdo drums.
- frontline: The front commission dances past in sequins, capes flaring open in a wave toward the stands.
- float: A float rolls through with a scarlet macaw spreading blue and gold mechanical wings, dancers on every tier.
- feathers: Samba dancers pass beneath towering fans of turquoise and emerald feathers.
- flag: The porta-bandeira spins in a gold hoop gown, the school's flag whirling above her, as the mestre-sala bows around her.
- confetti: The baianas pass in huge spinning skirts as confetti and streamers rain down.
- fireworks: Fireworks burst over Sugarloaf Mountain and the moonlit bay.
- dawn: The sky pinks over the bay, a sweeper in orange pushes the confetti away, and night falls again for the next school.
