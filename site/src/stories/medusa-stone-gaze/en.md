## legend

Far to the west, at the very edge of the world, lived the three Gorgons, daughters of the old gods of the sea. Two of them, Stheno and Euryale, could never die. The third, Medusa, was mortal.

The Roman poet Ovid tells that Medusa was once a beautiful young woman, and that her hair was the loveliest thing about her. Many suitors hoped to marry her. But Poseidon, god of the sea, seized her inside the temple of Athena. The goddess, enraged that her temple had been defiled, turned her fury on Medusa and changed that shining hair into a nest of hissing snakes. From then on, anyone who looked upon her face was turned to stone.

Medusa lived with her sisters at the edge of the world, and all around their home stood figures of men and beasts made of stone, caught forever in the moment they had met her eyes.

Then came the hero Perseus. King Polydectes, who wanted him out of the way, sent him to fetch the Gorgon's head, sure that he would never return. But the gods were on Perseus's side. Hermes gave him a curved blade of adamant. Nymphs gave him winged sandals, a magic bag and the cap of Hades, which made its wearer invisible. And Athena guided him with a bronze shield polished like a mirror.

Perseus flew to the Gorgons and found them asleep. Looking only at Medusa's reflection in the shield, never at her face, he swung the blade and cut off her head. From her neck sprang the winged horse Pegasus and the warrior Chrysaor, the children of Poseidon. Perseus thrust the head into his bag and flew off, while the two immortal sisters woke and hunted for him in vain.

Even in death, Medusa's eyes kept their power. On his way home Perseus turned the giant Atlas into a mountain, rescued the princess Andromeda from a sea monster, and at last held up the head before King Polydectes and his court, who became stone where they sat. Then he gave the head to Athena, and she set it on her aegis, where it strikes terror into her enemies.

The Greeks carved Medusa's face on shields, temples and doorways to ward off evil, and from ancient stones her snake-haired face still stares out at us today.

## beats

- idle: At dusk on the steps of a ruined Greek temple, Medusa waits while her snake hair writhes and hisses.
- crow: A crow flies in from the right, heading straight toward her face.
- gaze: Her eyes flash green and the crow turns to grey stone in mid-flight, like every creature that meets her gaze.
- fall: The stone crow drops from the air and shatters on the temple steps.
- settle: The snakes coil and settle, and Medusa is alone again among the ruins.
