---
origin: Spain · Miguel de Cervantes' novel Don Quixote, Part One, 1605

## legend

In a village of La Mancha, whose name the author would rather not recall, there lived a gentleman of nearly fifty, lean and gaunt, who read so many books of chivalry that his wits dried up. He decided to become a knight errant himself. He scoured his great-grandfather's rusty armour, patched a helmet with pasteboard, named his bony old nag Rocinante, and named himself Don Quixote de la Mancha. Every knight needs a lady, so he chose a farm girl from a nearby village and called her Dulcinea del Toboso.

For a squire he took a neighbour, a round and simple labourer named Sancho Panza, who came along on his donkey because the knight promised to make him governor of an island.

Soon they came to a plain with thirty or forty windmills on it. "Fortune is guiding us better than we could have wished," said Don Quixote. "Look there, friend Sancho: thirty or more monstrous giants. I mean to fight them all and kill them."

"What giants?" asked Sancho.

"Those you see there, with the long arms. Some have arms nearly two leagues long."

"Look, your worship," said Sancho, "those are not giants but windmills, and what look like arms are the sails that turn in the wind and make the millstone go."

But the knight would not listen. "Do not flee, cowards and vile creatures," he shouted, "for a single knight attacks you!" Commending himself to his lady Dulcinea, he set his lance and charged at Rocinante's full gallop at the first mill. Just then a breeze turned the sails. The lance struck a sail, the wind swung it round so hard that the lance broke to pieces, and horse and rider were dragged along and sent rolling across the plain.

Sancho came up as fast as his donkey could trot and found his master unable to move. "Didn't I tell your worship they were only windmills?"

"Hush, friend Sancho," said Don Quixote. "The fortunes of war change more than anything. I believe the enchanter Frestón, who stole my books, has turned these giants into windmills to rob me of the glory of defeating them. But in the end his evil arts will do little against my good sword."

Sancho helped him back onto Rocinante, whose shoulder was half out of joint, and they rode on toward Puerto Lápice, the knight grieving only for his broken lance.

## beats

- plain: At pink dawn the lanky knight rides in on bony Rocinante, lance upright, with round Sancho Panza behind on his donkey.
- giants: He lowers his lance, and in his eyes the mills shimmer into bearded, red-eyed giants while Sancho points and shakes his head.
- charge: He couches his lance and gallops at the nearest mill in a cloud of dust as Sancho waves his arms and shouts.
- sail: The lance jams in a turning sail, which hoists the knight round a full circle until the lance shatters and flings him off.
- fall: He tumbles through the air and lands flat on his back in the dust, seeing stars, as Sancho trots up.
- ride: The giants are windmills again; he blames an enchanter, climbs back on Rocinante and gallops off with Sancho.
